Password strength checker

10

A password input, a “Check Strength” button and a label. Score: length +10 per 2 characters (minimum 6 required), uppercase +15, lowercase +15, number +20, special !@#$%^&* +25. Levels: Very Weak 0–30, Weak 31–50, Medium 51–70, Strong 71–90, Very Strong 91+. At 100+, show “Perfect Password!”. Track attempts; after more than 8 attempts without reaching Strong, show “Need practice!” and tips.

@@table sample Input|Expected abc|Very Weak hello123|Weak Strong@Pass|Strong Perfect@Pass123|Perfect Password! @@end
Read the samples before the rule

If you give 10 points per 2 characters of the whole length (the lecture solution), hello123 scores 40 + 15 + 20 = 75 → Strong, but the sample says Weak. Strong@Pass would score 105 → Perfect, but the sample says Strong.

Counting only the characters beyond the 6-character minimum matches all four samples:

@@table checked against the samples Password|Len|Length pts floor((len−6)/2)×10|Upper|Lower|Digit|Special|Score|Level abc|3|0 (below 6)|—|15|—|—|15|Very Weak ✓ hello123|8|10|—|15|20|—|45|Weak ✓ Strong@Pass|11|20|15|15|—|25|75|Strong ✓ Perfect@Pass123|15|40|15|15|20|25|115|Perfect Password! ✓ @@end

Why compare by CategoryID

Part 2 renamed Bottle and Pen’s CategoryName to “Low Performing”, but their CategoryID (303) didn’t change. Linking the subquery on CategoryID keeps “its own category” stable. Here, linking on CategoryName gives the same labels, because only Bottle and Pen were renamed and they became their own group.

Revenue type

The original solution uses Revenue INT. 350000 × 1.10 = 385000 exactly, so INT works for this data. DECIMAL is safer for any 10% update.
